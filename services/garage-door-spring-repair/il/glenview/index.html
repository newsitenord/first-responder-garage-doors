A garage door spring is a counterweight under enormous tension: it makes a 150–250 lb door feel light enough to lift with one hand. When it breaks, the door becomes dangerously heavy, and the opener is suddenly asked to lift a weight it was never designed to take. In Glenview, a broken spring almost always announces itself with a loud bang from the garage.

What we handle in Glenview

Torsion springs run on a shaft above the door and are wound to counterbalance its weight; extension springs stretch along the horizontal tracks. Both have a rated cycle life — typically 10,000 for standard springs and 15,000–25,000 for high-cycle springs. A spring at the end of its cycle life breaks without warning, which is why replacing both springs as a pair is standard practice.

  • Door weight measured and spring size matched to it
  • Both springs replaced as a matched pair
  • Spring winding with proper winding bars and tension calibration
  • Cable, drum and bracket inspection while the spring is off
  • Door balance adjustment and opener force reset
  • Safety test and lubrication of the spring and bearings

When to call for spring repair in Glenview

  • A loud bang from the garage, often described as sounding like a gunshot
  • The door feels extremely heavy when lifted by hand
  • The opener strains, hums or trips its overload protection
  • A visible gap in the coil of a torsion spring
  • The door rises a few inches and stops
  • The door hangs unevenly or one side sags

Local conditions that affect doors in Glenview

When water gets into a track or behind a weather seal and refreezes overnight, the door can freeze to the slab. Forcing an opener through a frozen door is one of the most common causes of stripped gears and snapped lift cables we see in Cook County.

Cables and springs failing in cold snaps

Steel contracts in low temperatures and torsion springs that are near the end of their cycle life often break on the first hard freeze of the season.

Doors frozen to the floor

Meltwater under the bottom seal refreezes overnight, bonding the door to the concrete. The opener pulls against it and the trolley, gear or cable gives first.

Salt corrosion on hinges and rollers

Rusty hinges and dry nylon rollers create the grinding and shuddering that homeowners notice most in winter.

Track misalignment from frost heave

Ground movement and ice at the door opening can push a track out of plumb, which then drags on the panels.

Timing note: Book a pre-winter tune-up in early autumn: lubrication, balance check, spring cycle inspection and a bottom-seal test before the first freeze.

Door choice: Insulated steel or composite doors hold up best here; whatever the material, specify sealed bearings and corrosion-resistant hinges for a salt-exposed garage.

Before the technician arrives: Keep the area around the tracks clear and avoid storing items against the door panels. Bent tracks from stored ladders and bikes are one of the most common avoidable repairs.

Spring Repair in Glenview: common questions

Can I still use my door with a broken spring?

No. The opener will try to lift the full weight of the door and can burn out, strip its gears or snap a cable. Leave the door closed and call for service.

Why replace both springs if only one broke?

Both springs have cycled the same number of times. Replacing one leaves a spring of the same age that usually fails within a few months, which means a second service call.

How long do garage door springs last?

Standard springs are rated for roughly 10,000 cycles — about seven to ten years of normal daily use. High-cycle springs last two to three times longer.
